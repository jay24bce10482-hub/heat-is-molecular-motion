Temperature and kinetic energy

Temperature is a measure of how much translational kinetic energy the average molecule carries. The Boltzmann constant, kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K, converts between kelvin and joules.

⟨½ m v²⟩ = (f / 2) · kB T f is the number of directions a molecule can move in: 3 for a real gas (so 3/2 kBT), 2 for the flat world in this simulator (so kBT).

Mean kinetic energy against temperature

Dots are measured from the simulated molecules, the line is theory. Move the slider or press Sweep to add dots.

Mean speed against temperature

The curve bends because speed grows with the square root of temperature.

Energy rises in step with temperature. Double the temperature and the average kinetic energy doubles, which is why the dots on the left fall on a straight line through zero.

Speed rises more slowly. Kinetic energy goes with v², so speed goes with √T. Doubling the temperature makes a typical molecule only about 41% faster, and heating from 300 K to 1000 K speeds it up by a factor of 1.8.

Temperature describes the crowd, not any one molecule. At any instant the molecules cover a wide range of speeds, and every collision reshuffles energy between them. Watch the ringed molecule: it speeds up and slows down constantly, while the overall histogram barely changes.

The fast tail grows fastest. Drag the speed threshold far into the tail and compare 300 K with 600 K. The share of molecules beyond it can grow several-fold while the average speed rises by 41%. That is why chemical reactions and evaporation, which need molecules above some energy, speed up so sharply when things get warm.

Mass changes speed, not energy. Switch from helium to xenon at the same temperature. The average kinetic energy is identical, but the lighter atoms move √(131/4) ≈ 5.7 times faster.

This is a small molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) follows every particle in a system by applying Newton's laws in many tiny time steps. Nobody sets the speed distribution. It emerges from the collisions, and temperature is something you measure from the particles' kinetic energy: T = 2·KE / (f · N · kB) summed over all particles.

The simulator does exactly that. Each step moves every molecule in a straight line, finds the pairs that touch, and exchanges momentum along the line between their centres. When you move the temperature slider, it rescales all velocities by √(Tnew / Told), the simplest kind of thermostat. Production MD codes use the same ingredients with more realistic physics:

The core loop of a real MD code looks like this (velocity Verlet):

for (step = 0; step < nSteps; step++) {
  for (i of atoms) v[i] += 0.5 * dt * F[i] / m;   // half kick from the old forces
  for (i of atoms) r[i] += dt * v[i];              // drift to new positions
  F = lennardJonesForces(r);                       // recompute forces from the potential
  for (i of atoms) v[i] += 0.5 * dt * F[i] / m;   // half kick from the new forces
  if (step % 10 === 0) {
    T = 2 * kineticEnergy(v) / (3 * N * kB);       // temperature from KE, as in the tiles above
  }
}

Hard-disk and hard-sphere collisions are the limit of a very steep repulsive force, and the first computer simulations of liquids in the 1950s used exactly this model. Things to try with it here: the periodic option (molecules leaving one side re-enter on the other, as in an MD box), the one-speed restart (relaxation to equilibrium), and the sweep (an automated temperature scan, the way MD is used to map out properties).

Simplifications: molecules are drawn as disks that are far larger relative to the box than real ones, and playback is slowed so that you can follow it; real nitrogen at room conditions collides billions of times per second. Speeds in m/s are real values for the chosen gas and temperature, using the 2D Maxwell–Boltzmann formulas (mean speed √(π kBT / 2m), RMS speed √(2 kBT / m)).
